The Argument

The difference between DOT roads and theme parks is not technology. It is accountability. Theme parks have no sovereign immunity. Every injury faces a jury. DOTs kill with impunity.

DOT Roads — Sovereign Immunity

"I complied with the rule, so I am protected." That is the defense. It does not matter that the rule kills people. It does not matter that the road design is known to be dangerous. It does not matter that safer alternatives exist. If the DOT followed its own standards, no jury will hear the case. The dead are a cost of compliance.

Theme Parks — No Immunity

Every injury is subject to the judgment of a jury. The operator and builder are responsible to preempt even the normal foolish behaviors of children. You cannot hide behind a rule. You must actually be safe. The result: 3.7 injuries per million visitors. 3,000 times safer than DOT roads.

Sovereign immunity does not make roads safe. It makes DOTs unaccountable. Remove the immunity and every road design decision faces the same test as a roller coaster: will a jury agree this was safe enough?

The way to have safe government is not to trust it all to one, but to divide it among the many, distributing to every one exactly the functions he is competent to. Let the national government be entrusted with the defence of the nation, and its foreign and federal relations; the State governments with the civil rights, laws, police, and administration of what concerns the State generally; the counties with the local concerns of the counties, and each ward direct the interests within itself.

Usufruct

To use for profit without harm; to return in better condition for Posterity. Roads that kill 40,000 Americans a year violate the oldest principle of responsible stewardship. The Constitution's Preamble mandates promoting the "general Welfare" and securing "the Blessings of Liberty to ourselves and our Posterity." Sovereign immunity shields the violation. Remove it and usufruct becomes enforceable — every road must leave the community better than it found it.

Divided Sovereignty

Jefferson knew that concentrating power creates the conditions for abuse. Federal highway policy concentrated transportation decisions in Washington — removing them from the states, counties, and communities who bear the consequences. The DOT that designs a dangerous intersection in Tulsa is protected by federal sovereign immunity from Tulsa's jury. That is the opposite of divided sovereignty. That is the path to war the Framers tried to prevent.

Why It Works

Theme park regulations work because they harness the Wisdom of the Many. Every injury faces a jury of citizens. No bureaucrat decides what is "safe enough." The people decide.

The Wisdom of the Many

A jury is the Wisdom of the Many applied to safety. Twelve citizens, no expertise required, judging whether the operator did enough to protect people — including from their own foolishness. This is why theme parks design for the worst case: a child doing something stupid. DOTs design for the average case: an adult paying attention. The child dies. The DOT is protected.

Compliance vs Accountability

DOT sovereign immunity creates a compliance culture: follow the standard, avoid the lawsuit. Theme park liability creates an accountability culture: prevent the injury, regardless of what the standard says. Compliance asks "did we follow the rule?" Accountability asks "is anyone hurt?" These produce radically different outcomes — 3,000x different.

What DOTs Fight

DOTs fight theme park regulations because there is no sovereign immunity. Every design decision becomes subject to a jury. Every intersection where a child is killed becomes a question: did the designer do enough to prevent this? The answer, 11,200 injuries per million, is indefensible before a jury. That is why they fight. That is why it works.

Preempt Foolishness

Under theme park regulations, the operator must preempt even the normal foolish behaviors of children. A child will run. A child will not look. A child will do the wrong thing. The system must protect them anyway. DOT roads punish the child for being a child. Theme parks protect the child for being a child. That is the 3,000x difference.

21 Presidential Veto Messages

Nine presidents over 50 years vetoed federal road and canal bills on constitutional grounds. Each veto message confirmed the same principle: the Constitution does not grant Congress power over internal improvements. These are not opinions — they are official acts of constitutional enforcement.

An act to set apart and pledge certain funds for internal improvements…pledges funds 'for constructing roads and canals' does not appear that the power proposed to be exercised by the bill is among the enumerated powers.

— President James Madison, Bonus Bill veto, March 3, 1817

Sovereignty for all the purposes of internal improvement…is said to be derived: First, from the right to establish post-offices and post-roads; second, from the right to declare war; third, to regulate commerce; fourth, to pay the debts and provide for the common defense and general welfare; fifth, from the power to make all laws necessary and proper for carrying into execution all the powers vested by the Constitution… According to my judgment it can not be derived from either of those powers, nor from all of them united, and in consequence it does not exist.

— President James Monroe, Cumberland Road veto, May 4, 1822

It does not, in my opinion, possess it; and no bill, therefore, which admits it can receive my official sanction.

— President Andrew Jackson, Maysville Road Bill veto, May 27, 1830

I cannot but regard the bill as asserting the right to apply the funds and credit of the Federal Government to works of internal improvement of a local character.

— President John Tyler, 1842

Many of the works provided for by the bill are, in my opinion, wholly local in their character, and it appears to me that the local interests which are sought to be promoted might be more appropriately, as well as more justly, provided for by the States immediately interested.

— President James K. Polk, 1847

I can find no authority in the Constitution for making appropriations of the public money for such purposes, and I cannot approve a bill which in my judgment violates that instrument.

— President Millard Fillmore, 1850

The practice of the Government has been far from uniform on this subject, but I might refer to a large number of acts to show that the attention of Congress has been constantly turned to the local interests and convenience of certain ports.

— President Franklin Pierce, 1854

Among the powers expressly granted to Congress by the Constitution, there is no one which authorizes appropriations from the Treasury for internal improvements, for the purposes of commerce or otherwise, within the limits of a State.

— President James Buchanan, 1859

And Mr. Madison, in his veto message of the 3d March, 1817, declares that — 'The power to regulate commerce among the several States can not include a power to construct roads and canals.' What a vast field would the exercise of this power open for jobbing and corruption!

— President James Buchanan, St. Clair Flats veto, 1860

The Constitution does not expressly give Congress the power to make appropriations for purposes of this kind, and I am not aware that it possesses such power unless by implication.

— President Andrew Johnson, 1867
